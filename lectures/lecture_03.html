Algebraic method · Strategy

Pieper’s idea: eliminate joints in a deliberate order

Package the geometry first; do not expand three transforms into a page of trigonometry.

1 · Remove \(\theta_1\)

Translate by \(d_1\), then keep radial distance and height—quantities unchanged by rotation about \(z_0\).

2 · Remove \(\theta_2\)

Represent its action as a 2D rotation and use preservation of planar length.

3 · Solve \(\theta_3\)

Use \(t=\tan(\theta_3/2)\) to convert the trigonometric closure into a polynomial.

Guiding question: which scalar quantities remain unchanged when a point is rotated?

Algebraic method · Package joints 2 and 3

Isolate all \(\theta_3\)-dependence in one vector

Let \(c_i=\cos\theta_i\), \(s_i=\sin\theta_i\), \(c_{\alpha i}=\cos\alpha_i\), and \(s_{\alpha i}=\sin\alpha_i\).

Before rotation by \(\theta_2\), define

\[ \mathbf u(\theta_3)= \begin{bmatrix}a_2\\0\\d_2\end{bmatrix} +R_x(\alpha_2) \begin{bmatrix}a_3c_3\\a_3s_3\\d_3\end{bmatrix}. \]
\[ \begin{aligned} u_x&=a_2+a_3c_3,\\ u_y&=c_{\alpha2}a_3s_3-s_{\alpha2}d_3,\\ u_z&=d_2+s_{\alpha2}a_3s_3+c_{\alpha2}d_3. \end{aligned} \]

Each component is at most linear in \(s_3,c_3\).

Apply it · custom_3R \(\mathbf u(\theta_3)\)

At \(\theta_3=60^\circ\), the packaged vector is numerical

\[ \begin{aligned} u_x&=2+1.5\cos60^\circ=2.750,\\ u_y&=-0.25,\\ u_z&=1.25+1.5\sin60^\circ=2.549. \end{aligned} \]

Algebraic method · Eliminate \(\theta_1\)

Subtract \(d_1\), then keep radius and height

Translation by \(d_1\) places the target relative to the first joint’s axial offset.

\[\bar z=z_e-d_1,\qquad R=x^2+y^2\]

With \(\bar{\mathbf p}=[x\;y\;\bar z]^T\),

\[ \bar{\mathbf p}=R_z(\theta_1) \left( \begin{bmatrix}a_1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix} +R_x(\alpha_1)R_z(\theta_2)\mathbf u \right). \]
A rotation \(R_z(\theta_1)\) changes azimuth, but cannot change \(R\) or \(\bar z\). Therefore \(\theta_1\) is gone.

Algebraic method · Expose \(\theta_2\)

Name the two coordinates rotated by \(\theta_2\)

The pair \((U,V)\) is simply \((u_x,u_y)\) rotated through \(\theta_2\).

\[ \begin{bmatrix}U\\V\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}c_2&-s_2\\s_2&c_2\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}u_x\\u_y\end{bmatrix} \]
Invariant: \(U^2+V^2=u_x^2+u_y^2\).

After the twist \(\alpha_1\), the coordinates before \(R_z(\theta_1)\) are

\[ v_x=a_1+U,\quad v_y=c_{\alpha1}V-s_{\alpha1}u_z,\quad \bar z=s_{\alpha1}V+c_{\alpha1}u_z. \]

Apply it · custom_3R planar rotation

\(\theta_2=-45^\circ\) rotates \((u_x,u_y)\) into \((U,V)\)

\[ \begin{bmatrix}U\\V\end{bmatrix} =R(-45^\circ) \begin{bmatrix}2.750\\-0.250\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1.7678\\-2.1213\end{bmatrix} \]

Algebraic method · Recover \(V\)

The height equation gives one rotated coordinate

For the generic case \(s_{\alpha1}\neq0\), solve the \(\bar z\)-equation directly.

\[ \boxed{V(\theta_3)= \frac{\bar z-c_{\alpha1}u_z(\theta_3)}{s_{\alpha1}}} \]

Once \(\theta_3\) is known, \(V\) is known.

The other transverse component simplifies to

\[v_y=\frac{c_{\alpha1}\bar z-u_z}{s_{\alpha1}}.\]

Substitute \(V\) into \(v_y=c_{\alpha1}V-s_{\alpha1}u_z\).

Genericity condition: this route divides by \(s_{\alpha1}\); parallel axes need another elimination.

Algebraic method · Recover \(U\)

The radial equation gives the other coordinate

Use \(R=v_x^2+v_y^2\), then simplify before solving.

A

\(R=(a_1+U)^2+v_y^2=a_1^2+2a_1U+U^2+v_y^2\).

B

Replace \(U^2\) by \(u_x^2+u_y^2-V^2\).

C

Use the cancellation \(V^2-v_y^2=\bar z^2-u_z^2\).

\[ \boxed{U(\theta_3)=\frac{D(\theta_3)}{2a_1}}, \qquad D=R+\bar z^2-a_1^2-\|\mathbf u(\theta_3)\|^2 \]
Genericity condition: this step divides by \(a_1\). The case \(a_1=0\) motivates our geometric method.

Algebraic method · Eliminate \(\theta_2\)

Rotation preserves length—so \(\theta_2\) disappears

Both \(U\) and \(V\) are now functions of \(\theta_3\) and known data. Enforce the identity they must satisfy.

\[ \boxed{ F(\theta_3):= \frac{D(\theta_3)^2}{4a_1^2} +\left(\frac{\bar z-c_{\alpha1}u_z(\theta_3)}{s_{\alpha1}}\right)^2 -u_x(\theta_3)^2-u_y(\theta_3)^2=0} \]
No \(\theta_1\)

removed by radial and height invariants

No \(\theta_2\)

removed by \(U^2+V^2=u_x^2+u_y^2\)

This scalar closure equation contains only \(\theta_3\).

Algebraic method · Why degree four?

The tangent-half-angle substitution makes a quartic

\(\mathbf u\) is linear in \(s_3,c_3\), while \(\|\mathbf u\|^2\) also reduces to a linear expression using \(s_3^2+c_3^2=1\). Thus \(F\) is at most quadratic in \(s_3,c_3\).

Trig closure
\(F(s_3,c_3)=0\)

degree \(\le2\)

→
Rational parameter
\(t=\tan(\theta_3/2)\)

\(s_3=\frac{2t}{1+t^2}\)
\(c_3=\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}\)

→
Polynomial
\(b_4t^4+b_3t^3+b_2t^2+b_1t+b_0=0\)

at most four roots

Map every real root back with \(\theta_3=2\operatorname{atan}(t)\). Check \(\theta_3=\pi\) separately: it corresponds to \(t=\infty\).

Algebraic method · Closed form

A generic 3R position IK is algebraically closed-form

Polynomials through degree four have formulas in radicals. Since generic 3R position IK reduces to a quartic, it is analytically solvable without iteration.

Closed form gives

  • a finite algebraic procedure;
  • enumeration of all candidates;
  • no initial guess.

It does not imply

  • every target is reachable;
  • every root is real;
  • radical formulas are numerically best.
In practice: form the quartic, use a stable polynomial root solver, then validate candidates with forward kinematics.

Algebraic method · Backpropagation 1/2

Backpropagate each root to \(\theta_2\)

\[\begin{bmatrix}U\\V\end{bmatrix}=R(\theta_2)\begin{bmatrix}u_x\\u_y\end{bmatrix}\]

Subtract their polar angles.

\[\boxed{\theta_2=\operatorname{atan2}(V,U)-\operatorname{atan2}(u_y,u_x)}\]

Wrap to the chosen joint interval.

If \(u_x=u_y=0\), joint 2 is not identifiable from this position constraint.

Algebraic method · Degeneracies

The quartic route is generic—not universal as written

A zero divisor signals additional geometry. It does not mean there is no IK; it means this elimination path is inappropriate.

\(a_1=0\)

The common-normal distance between axes 1 and 2 vanishes: the axes intersect.

\[U=D/(2a_1)\quad\text{is invalid.}\]

Opportunity: their intersection is fixed by both rotations, enabling a geometric decomposition.

\(\alpha_1=0\) or \(\pi\)

Axes 1 and 2 are parallel, so \(s_{\alpha1}=0\).

\[V=(\bar z-c_{\alpha1}u_z)/s_{\alpha1}\quad\text{is invalid.}\]

Opportunity: parallel-axis geometry permits a different, usually lower-degree reduction.

See the degeneracy · a₁ → 0

As the common normal collapses, the axes intersect

The animation continuously moves the custom_3R second axis toward the first. At intersection, division by \(a_1\) fails—but a shared fixed point appears.

The complete Pieper pipeline

Method 2 · Geometry

Paden–Kahan Subproblems

A revolute joint draws a circle. IK asks where that circle meets another constraint.

Geometric method · Circle primitive

Rotation about an axis traces a circle

Let \(\boldsymbol\omega\) be a unit axis through the origin and decompose a point into axial and perpendicular parts.

\[ \mathbf p_\parallel=(\boldsymbol\omega^T\mathbf p)\boldsymbol\omega, \qquad \mathbf p_\perp=\mathbf p-\mathbf p_\parallel \]
center\(\mathbf p_\parallel\)
radius\(r=\|\mathbf p_\perp\|\)
plane\(\boldsymbol\omega^T\mathbf x=\boldsymbol\omega^T\mathbf p\)
Rotation preserves axial coordinate, distance to the axis, and Euclidean length.

Geometric method · Circle–circle distance

Before solving an angle, count the intersections

For coplanar circles with radii \(r_1,r_2\) and center distance \(d\), feasibility is determined by three lengths.

\(d>r_1+r_2\)

too far apart

\(d=r_1+r_2\)

external tangent

\(|r_1-r_2|<d<r_1+r_2\)

two intersections

\(d<|r_1-r_2|\)

one inside the other

Geometric method · PK1 statement

PK1 rotates one point onto another

Given unit axis \(\boldsymbol\omega\) and points \(\mathbf p,\mathbf q\), find \(\theta\) such that \(R(\boldsymbol\omega,\theta)\mathbf p=\mathbf q\).

PK1
\[R(\boldsymbol\omega,\theta)\mathbf p=\mathbf q\]

Exact feasibility requires

\[ \boldsymbol\omega^T\mathbf p=\boldsymbol\omega^T\mathbf q, \qquad \|\mathbf p_\perp\|=\|\mathbf q_\perp\|. \]
If feasible and the orbit radius is nonzero, PK1 has one solution modulo \(2\pi\).

Geometric method · PK1 solution

PK1 is an oriented angle in the orbit plane

Project both points perpendicular to the axis; then compute cosine and signed sine of the required angle.

\[ \mathbf p_\perp=(I-\boldsymbol\omega\boldsymbol\omega^T)\mathbf p,\qquad \mathbf q_\perp=(I-\boldsymbol\omega\boldsymbol\omega^T)\mathbf q \]
\[ \cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf p_\perp^T\mathbf q_\perp}{\|\mathbf p_\perp\|\|\mathbf q_\perp\|},\quad \sin\theta=\frac{\boldsymbol\omega^T(\mathbf p_\perp\times\mathbf q_\perp)}{\|\mathbf p_\perp\|\|\mathbf q_\perp\|}. \]
\[ \boxed{\theta=\operatorname{atan2}\!\left( \boldsymbol\omega^T(\mathbf p_\perp\times\mathbf q_\perp), \mathbf p_\perp^T\mathbf q_\perp\right)} \]

\(\operatorname{atan2}\)(sine-like, cosine-like) preserves direction and all four quadrants.

If \(\|\mathbf p_\perp\|=0\), the point lies on the axis and does not move: the angle is arbitrary or the constraint is infeasible.

Geometric method · PK3 statement

PK3 rotates a point to a prescribed distance

Given \(\boldsymbol\omega,\mathbf p,\mathbf q,\delta\), find every angle for which the rotated point lies on a sphere centered at \(\mathbf q\).

PK3
\[\|R(\boldsymbol\omega,\theta)\mathbf p-\mathbf q\|=\delta\]

The orbit is one circle. The distance constraint is a sphere. Slice the sphere by the orbit plane to obtain a second circle.

Geometric method · PK3 reduction

Slice the sphere by the orbit plane

Axial separation consumes part of the allowed distance; the remainder becomes an in-plane circle radius.

\[ \mathbf c=(\boldsymbol\omega^T\mathbf p)\boldsymbol\omega,\quad \mathbf p_\perp=\mathbf p-\mathbf c,\quad r=\|\mathbf p_\perp\| \]
\[ h=\boldsymbol\omega^T(\mathbf q-\mathbf c),\quad \mathbf q_\perp=(I-\boldsymbol\omega\boldsymbol\omega^T)(\mathbf q-\mathbf c),\quad d=\|\mathbf q_\perp\|. \]

Cross-section radius:

\[\boxed{\delta_\perp=\sqrt{\delta^2-h^2}}\]

First check: \(\delta^2-h^2\ge0\).

Second check: \(|r-\delta_\perp|\le d\le r+\delta_\perp\).

A 3D circle–sphere intersection is now an ordinary 2D circle–circle intersection.

Geometric method · PK3 solution

The law of cosines gives the two branches

Let \(\gamma\) rotate \(\mathbf p_\perp\) toward the second circle’s center. Let \(\phi\) be the triangle’s remaining angle.

\[ \gamma=\operatorname{atan2}\!\left( \boldsymbol\omega^T(\mathbf p_\perp\times\mathbf q_\perp), \mathbf p_\perp^T\mathbf q_\perp\right) \]
\[ \phi=\arccos\!\left(\frac{r^2+d^2-\delta_\perp^2}{2rd}\right) \]
\[\boxed{\theta=\gamma\pm\phi}\]
two\(0<\phi<\pi\)
one\(\phi=0\) or \(\pi\): tangent
noneacos argument outside \([-1,1]\)
Clamp within floating-point tolerance before \(\arccos\); reject larger feasibility violations.

Geometric method · Connect to D–H

a₁ = 0 gives the fixed point we need

In standard D–H geometry, \(a_1\) is the common-normal distance between the first two joint axes. If \(a_1=0\), axes 1 and 2 intersect at \(\mathbf c\).

\[ e^{\widehat{\xi}_1\theta_1}\mathbf c=\mathbf c,\qquad e^{\widehat{\xi}_2\theta_2}\mathbf c=\mathbf c \]

Both rotations fix \(\mathbf c\). Distances from \(\mathbf c\) are independent of \(\theta_1,\theta_2\).

The algebraic degeneracy becomes a geometric advantage.

Geometric 3R IK · Setup

Write the required displacement as three joint motions

Let \(g_d\) be the desired tool pose and \(g_0\) the home pose. Define \(g=g_dg_0^{-1}\).

\[ e^{\widehat\xi_1\theta_1} e^{\widehat\xi_2\theta_2} e^{\widehat\xi_3\theta_3}=g \]
\(\mathbf c\)

intersection of axes 1 and 2

\(\mathbf p\)

a convenient point not on axis 3

\(\mathbf r\)

on axis 2, but not on axis 1

\(\mathbf s\)

a point not on axis 2

Reachability assumption: \(g_d\) lies on this 3-DOF chain’s pose manifold. A rotation leaves points on its own axis fixed, so strategic point choices cancel unknown exponentials.

Geometric 3R IK · Step 1

Solve \(\theta_3\) with PK3

Apply the displacement equation to \(\mathbf p\), subtract the common intersection \(\mathbf c\), and take norms.

A

\(e^{\widehat\xi_1\theta_1}e^{\widehat\xi_2\theta_2}e^{\widehat\xi_3\theta_3}\mathbf p=g\mathbf p\).

B

Subtract \(\mathbf c\). Rotations 1 and 2 fix \(\mathbf c\), so they rotate the difference vector.

C

Take norms; the first two rotations preserve length and disappear.

\[ \boxed{\|e^{\widehat\xi_3\theta_3}\mathbf p-\mathbf c\| =\|g\mathbf p-\mathbf c\|=:\delta} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{PK3} \]
The circle–sphere intersection produces zero, one, or two candidates for \(\theta_3\).

Geometric 3R IK · Step 2

For each \(\theta_3\), isolate the first two rotations

Move the known third-joint motion to the right.

\[ h(\theta_3):=g\,e^{-\widehat\xi_3\theta_3},\qquad e^{\widehat\xi_1\theta_1}e^{\widehat\xi_2\theta_2}=h. \]
Next cancellation: which point makes the second exponential disappear?
\[ \mathbf r\text{ on axis 2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad e^{\widehat\xi_2\theta_2}\mathbf r=\mathbf r. \]

Geometric 3R IK · Step 3

Solve \(\theta_1\) with PK1

Apply the two-rotation equation to \(\mathbf r\), chosen on axis 2 but not on axis 1.

\[ e^{\widehat\xi_1\theta_1} \underbrace{e^{\widehat\xi_2\theta_2}\mathbf r}_{\mathbf r} =h\mathbf r \]

The second rotation is cancelled by the point choice.

\[ \boxed{e^{\widehat\xi_1\theta_1}\mathbf r=h\mathbf r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{PK1} \]

Use the PK1 \(\operatorname{atan2}\) formula about axis 1.

Choosing \(\mathbf r\) off axis 1 gives a nonzero orbit radius, so \(\theta_1\) is observable.

Geometric 3R IK · Step 4

Finally solve \(\theta_2\) with PK1

Remove the known first rotation and apply the result to any point \(\mathbf s\) not on axis 2.

\[e^{\widehat\xi_2\theta_2}=e^{-\widehat\xi_1\theta_1}h\]

Apply both sides to \(\mathbf s\).

\[ \boxed{e^{\widehat\xi_2\theta_2}\mathbf s =e^{-\widehat\xi_1\theta_1}h\mathbf s} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{PK1} \]

Use the same oriented-angle formula about axis 2.

The 3R IK has become one PK3 solve followed by two PK1 solves.

Synthesis

Algebra and geometry solve the same constraint differently

Pieper / D–H elimination

  • generic \(a_1\neq0,\ s_{\alpha1}\neq0\);
  • invariants eliminate \(\theta_1,\theta_2\);
  • a quartic enumerates \(\theta_3\);
  • backpropagate with \(\operatorname{atan2}\).

Paden–Kahan decomposition

  • exploits \(a_1=0\), intersecting axes;
  • fixed points cancel joint motions;
  • circle intersections expose feasibility;
  • PK3 → PK1 → PK1.
Common principle: eliminate a rotation with something it cannot change—distance, axial projection, or a point on its axis.

Takeaways

Four ideas to keep

1

\(R=x^2+y^2\) and \(\bar z=z_e-d_1\) eliminate \(\theta_1\).

2

Planar length eliminates \(\theta_2\), leaving a quartic in \(\tan(\theta_3/2)\).

3

Backpropagation subtracts polar angles with \(\operatorname{atan2}\).

4

For \(a_1=0\), the axes’ intersection gives PK3 → PK1 → PK1.
